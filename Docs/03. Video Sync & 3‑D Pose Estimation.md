## 1.  Introduction & System Overview

This pipeline converts **two unsynchronized RGB videos** of a subject (a **front** view and a **side** view) into a **single temporally‑coherent, root‑centered 3‑D skeletal sequence** with consistent bone lengths across all frames.
The pipeline is organized into four computational stages
```
┌────────────────┐     ┌────────────────┐
│  front.mp4     │     │  side.mp4      │
└────────┬───────┘     └────────┬───────┘
         │                      │
     [YOLO pose]           [YOLO pose]
         │                      │
         ▼                      ▼
  front_keypoints.json    side_keypoints.json
         │                      │
         └── cross‑correlate ───┘
                    │
                    ▼
            Δt  (side lag in frames)
                    │
          ┌─────────┴──────────┐
          ▼                    ▼
      synced front          synced side
          │                    │
          └─── Bayesian MAP ───┘
                    │
                    ▼
            X ∈ ℝ^(T × 17 × 3)
   fused confidence, global bone lengths
```

At a high level the algorithm,
1. Uses **YOLO11x‑pose** to detect 17 COCO keypoints per frame in each video.
2. Estimates the inter‑camera frame lag using **cross‑correlation of a 1‑D motion signal** derived from the keypoints.
3. Fuses the two synchronized 2‑D streams into 3‑D via a **batch Bayesian Maximum A Posteriori (MAP)** estimator solved with **SciPy `least_squares`** inside an **EM loop** that updates shared bone lengths.
4. Optionally renders skeleton‑annotated videos, a side‑by‑side synced video, and an interactive Plotly viewer comparing raw vs. reprojected 2‑D poses.

## 2.  2‑D Keypoint Extraction (YOLO Pose)

### 2.1.  Model & Output

The pipeline uses `yolo11x-pose.pt`, the largest pose variant of Ultralytics YOLO11, which predicts
- A bounding box per person
- **17 COCO keypoints**, each with a pixel coordinate $(x, y)$ and confidence $c \in [0,1]$

The 17 keypoint indices (`KEYPOINT_NAMES`) are

| **idx** | **name**       | **idx** | **name**                                   |
| ------- | -------------- | ------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| 0       | nose           | 9       | left_wrist                                 |
| 1       | left_eye       | 10      | right_wrist                                |
| 2       | right_eye      | 11      | **<font color="#f79646">left_hip</font>**  |
| 3       | left_ear       | 12      | <font color="#f79646">**right_hip**</font> |
| 4       | right_ear      | 13      | left_knee                                  |
| 5       | left_shoulder  | 14      | right_knee                                 |
| 6       | right_shoulder | 15      | left_ankle                                 |
| 7       | left_elbow     | 16      | right_ankle                                |
| 8       | right_elbow    |         |                                            |
Joints 11 and 12 (the hips) form the **root** and are treated specially during normalization.

### 2.2.  Per‑Frame Extraction

For every frame `t` the pipeline records, 
$$\mathbf{p}_{j,t} \in \mathbb{R}^2, \quad c_{j,t} \in [0,1], \qquad j \in \{0,\dots,16\}$$
Missing detections are stored as `NaN` for coordinates and `0.0` for confidence, preserving the temporal grid so that `frame_idx` in the DataFrame directly corresponds to the physical frame number.
## 3.  Temporal Synchronization of Multi‑View Video

Because the front and side cameras are started independently, their streams differ by an **unknown integer frame offset** $\Delta t$. We estimate this offset by aligning a scalar motion signal extracted from each view.
### 3.1.  Motion Signal Construction

For each view, define a scalar per‑frame "movement energy"
$$m_t \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{17} \bigl\lVert \mathbf{p}_{j,t} - \mathbf{p}_{j,t-1} \bigr\rVert_2, \qquad t = 1, 2, \dots, T-1$$
**Intuition:** When the subject moves (jumps, squats, lifts), all 17 joints displace simultaneously $m_t$ spikes. During rest, $m_t$ drops near zero. This creates a **characteristic 1‑D fingerprint** of the movement, dominated by gross body motion and independent of camera scale (since normalization follows).

**Missing‑data handling:** If a joint's coordinates are `NaN`, the implementation duplicates the previous frame's coordinates so that no spurious spike is introduced,
$$\mathbf{p}_{j,t} \leftarrow \mathbf{p}_{j,t-1} \quad \text{if } \mathbf{p}_{j,t} \text{ is invalid}.$$

### 3.2.  Standard Score Normalization

The two cameras observe the subject from very different distances, so the absolute magnitudes of $m_t$ differ. We normalize each signal to zero mean and unit variance

$$\hat{m}_t \;=\; \frac{m_t - \mu_m}{\sigma_m + 10^{-8}}$$
The small constant $10^{-8}$ avoids division by zero when the subject is perfectly still.
### 3.3.  Cross‑Correlation & Lag Estimation

Define the cross‑correlation between the front and side normalized signals
$$C[\tau] \;=\; \sum_{t} \hat{m}^{\text{front}}[t] \, \hat{m}^{\text{side}}[t - \tau]$$
This is computed in one shot with `scipy.signal.correlate(front, side, mode='full')`, which yields valid lag,
$$\tau \in \{-(L_{\text{side}}-1), \dots, L_{\text{front}}-1\}.$$
The **optimal lag** is the correlation peak
$$\Delta t \;=\; \arg\max_{\tau} C[\tau]$$
**Interpretation:**
- $\Delta t > 0$ → the side stream **lags** the front by $\Delta t$ frames; the front video starts $\Delta t$ frames earlier.
- $\Delta t < 0$ → the front stream lags the side by $\vert{}\Delta t\vert{}$ frames.
- $\Delta t = 0$ → streams already aligned.

The peak's **sharpness** reflects synchronization quality. a single dominant peak implies a confident estimate.

### 3.4.  Applying the Lag

The lag is stored as `video_sync_side_lag_frames` on the experiment entry. During fusion, the sign is inverted (see §5.2) so that the two DataFrames begin at the same physical moment
$$\text{frame\_lag} = -\, \Delta t_{\text{stored}}.$$

Then
- If `frame_lag ≥ 0`, drop the first `frame_lag` rows of the **side** sequence.
- If `frame_lag < 0`, drop the first `|frame_lag|` rows of the **front** sequence.
- Trim to `min(len(front), len(side))` so both views have identical frame count.

**Diagnostic Plots**
When `plot=True`, the routine renders two Plotly figures:
1. **Raw unsynchronized** normalized movement signals overlaid.
2. **Aligned** signals with the side stream shifted by `best_lag`, showing peak overlap.
## 4.  Bayesian MAP 3‑D Pose Fusion

### 4.1.  Coordinate Convention

After root‑centering and scale normalization the two views are interpreted as <font color="#f79646">**orthographic projections**</font> of the same 3‑D world

| **Camera** | **Observes 2‑D coordinate** | **World axis**                |
| ---------- | --------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Front      | $(u_f, v_f)$                | $X$ (lateral), $Y$ (vertical) |
| Side       | $(u_s, v_s)$                | $Z$ (depth), $Y$ (vertical)   |
Therefore
$$u_f \leftrightarrow X,\quad v_f \leftrightarrow Y,\quad u_s \leftrightarrow Z,\quad v_s \leftrightarrow Y.$$
Both cameras see the **same vertical axis $Y$**, which is the key redundancy that lets us constrain depth.
### 4.2.  Pre‑processing Steps

**(a) Root centering.** For each frame $t$ define the root as the midpoint of the hips,
$$\mathbf{r}_t = \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(\mathbf{p}_{11,t} + \mathbf{p}_{12,t}\right)$$
and shift every joint,
$$\mathbf{p}'_{j,t} = \mathbf{p}_{j,t} - \mathbf{r}_t.$$
If hip confidences drop below 0.1, fall back to the mean of all valid joints. The per‑frame root is stored for later reprojection.

**(b) Per‑view scale normalization.** Median bone length over a fixed set of long reference bones,
$$\mathcal{B}_{\text{ref}} = \{\,(11,13),\,(12,14),\,(13,15),\,(14,16)\,\} \quad\text{(hip‑knee, knee‑ankle)}$$
$$s \;=\; \frac{1}{\mathrm{median}\bigl\{\, \lVert \mathbf{p}'_{a,t} - \mathbf{p}'_{b,t} \rVert \;:\; (a,b)\in\mathcal{B}_{\text{ref}},\; t \,\bigr\}}$$
Then every joint is scaled by $s$,
$$\mathbf{p}''_{j,t} = s \cdot \mathbf{p}'_{j,t}.$$
Because the reference bones are among the longest in the body, this normalizes both cameras to a **comparable anatomical unit** despite different focal lengths / distances.

**(c) Validity masks.** A joint is considered valid when
$$\text{valid}_{j,t} = \neg\,\text{NaN}(\mathbf{p}_{j,t}) \;\wedge\; c_{j,t} > 0.05.$$
### 4.3.  The MAP Objective
Let the latent variables be
- $X_t \in \mathbb{R}^{17 \times 3}$ — the 3‑D joint positions at frame $t$
- $L_b \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ — the global bone length for each skeleton pair $b$

The joint cost function (negative log‑posterior) is,
  $$E(X, L) \;=\; E_{\text{reproj}} \;+\; \lambda_{\text{bone}} \, E_{\text{bone}} \;+\; \lambda_{\text{temp}} \, E_{\text{temp}} $$
#### 4.3.1.  Weighted Reprojection Error
$$E_{\text{reproj}} \;=\; \sum_{t}\sum_{j} c_{j,t}\, \Bigl[\,(X_{j,t}^x - u^f_{j,t})^2 + (X_{j,t}^y - v^f_{j,t})^2 + (X_{j,t}^z - u^s_{j,t})^2 + (X_{j,t}^y - v^s_{j,t})^2 \,\Bigr]$$
Notice

- The **front view** constrains $X$ and $Y$.
- The **side view** constrains $Z$ and the same $Y$.
- The vertical coordinate $Y$ appears in **both** terms, coupling the two views.

Because the residuals are multiplied by $\sqrt{c_{j,t}}$ inside `least_squares`, the squared objective is scaled by $c_{j,t}$ — YOLO‑confident joints are trusted more.
#### 4.3.2.  Anatomical Bone‑Length Prior
$$E_{\text{bone}} \;=\; \sum_{t}\sum_{(p_1,p_2) \in \text{Pairs}} \Bigl(\, \bigl\lVert X_{p_1,t} - X_{p_2,t} \bigr\rVert_2 \;-\; L_b \,\Bigr)^2$$
This enforces that a bone's Euclidean length in 3‑D matches a **single global estimate** $L_b$ across all frames. It is the primary constraint that resolves the $Z$ ambiguity: without it, many $(X,Y,Z)$ combinations would reproject to the same 2‑D points.

**Skeleton pairs** (COCO 17)
  $$\{(0,1),(0,2),(1,3),(2,4),(5,6),(5,7),(7,9),(6,8),(8,10),(5,11),(6,12),(11,12),(11,13),(13,15),(12,14),(14,16)\}$$
#### 4.3.3.  Temporal Smoothness (Constant‑Velocity Prior)
$$E_{\text{temp}} \;=\; \sum_{t=2}^{T}\sum_{j=1}^{17} \bigl\lVert X_{j,t} - 2X_{j,t-1} + X_{j,t-2} \bigr\rVert^2$$
This is the squared **second finite difference** — i.e., acceleration — and penalizes physically implausible high‑frequency jitter. It does not bias smooth, low‑acceleration motions.

#### 4.3.4.  Total Weighted Residual Vector
`least_squares` minimizes $\tfrac12\lVert f(\mathbf{X}) \rVert^2$, so we pass the square roots of the weights:

  

$$f(\mathbf{X}) = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{c}\,(X^x - u^f) \\ \sqrt{c}\,(X^y - v^f) \\ \sqrt{c}\,(X^z - u^s) \\ \sqrt{c}\,(X^y - v^s) \\ \sqrt{\lambda_{\text{temp}}}\,(X_t - 2X_{t-1} + X_{t-2}) \\ \sqrt{\lambda_{\text{bone}}}\,(\lVert X_{p_1}-X_{p_2}\rVert - L_b) \end{bmatrix}$$
so that $\tfrac12\lVert f\rVert^2 = \tfrac12(E_{\text{reproj}} + \lambda_{\text{bone}} E_{\text{bone}} + \lambda_{\text{temp}} E_{\text{temp}})$.
### 4.4.  Optimization Strategy - Batch EM

Bone lengths are unknown a priori, so we alternate between two steps,

**E‑step (Solve poses, fix $L$)**
Solve for all $T \cdot 17 \cdot 3$ coordinates with `scipy.optimize.least_squares`

|**Setting**|**Rationale**|
|---|---|
|`method='trf'`|Trust Region Reflective — bounded, stable, sparse‑friendly|
|`loss='soft_l1'`|Huber‑like robust loss; down‑weights outlier YOLO detections|
|`f_scale=5.0`|Residual scale above which the loss becomes linear|
|`x_scale='jac'`|Auto‑scaled variables for numerical conditioning|
|`jac_sparsity`|Custom CSR pattern → sparse finite differences|

**M‑step (Update bones, fix $X$)**
Recompute each bone's global length as the **median** of its measured length over all frames:

$$L_b \;\leftarrow\; \operatorname{median}_{t}\Bigl\lVert X_{p_1,t} - X_{p_2,t} \Bigr\rVert$$
Median (rather than mean) adds robustness to outlier frames.

**Convergence** 
For each EM iteration the change $\Delta L = \max_b \vert{}L_b^{(k)} - L_b^{(k-1)}\vert{}$ is printed. With `n_em_iters = 3` the pipeline converges in practice.
### 4.5.  Jacobian Sparsity Matrix

A naive dense Jacobian would have shape $(4TJ + 3(T-2)J + TP)\times(3TJ)$, which grows extremely fast. Because each residual only touches a handful of unknowns, we build a sparse CSR mask with the exact nonzero pattern:

|**Residual block**|**Rows**|**Nonzero columns per row**|
|---|---|---|
|Front‑X|$TJ$|1 (`X^x`)|
|Front‑Y|$TJ$|1 (`X^y`)|
|Side‑Z|$TJ$|1 (`X^z`)|
|Side‑Y|$TJ$|1 (`X^y`)|
|Temporal|$3(T-2)J$|3 (frame $t,t\!-\!1,t\!-\!2$)|
|Bone|$TP$|6 ($p_1,p_2$ × 3 coords)|

Total rows,
$$n_{\text{res}} = 4TJ + 3(T-2)J + TP.$$
Total parameters,
$$n_{\text{par}} = 3TJ.$$
This typically delivers **one to two orders of magnitude** speed‑up in finite‑difference Jacobian evaluation.

### 4.6.  Initialization

**(a) 3‑D seed $X_0$**
$$X_0^x = u^f,\qquad X_0^z = u^s,\qquad X_0^y = \frac{w_f\, v^f + w_s\, v^s}{w_f + w_s + \epsilon},$$
where $w_f, w_s$ are validity masks. The $Y$ blend ensures joints seen by only one camera still receive a sensible vertical estimate.

**(b) Bone seed $L_b^{(0)}$**
$$L_b^{(0)} = \max\Bigl(\, \operatorname{median}_t \lVert X_{p_1,t}-X_{p_2,t}\rVert^{(f)},\; \operatorname{median}_t \lVert X_{p_1,t}-X_{p_2,t}\rVert^{(s)} \,\Bigr)$$
i.e., the larger of the two single‑view medians. Taking the max avoids under‑estimating depth‑projected foreshortened bones.
### 4.7.  Confidence Fusion

For each joint, the fused confidence is the clipped sum of the two view confidences
$$c^{\text{fused}}_{j,t} \;=\; \mathrm{clip}\!\left(c^f_{j,t} + c^s_{j,t},\; 0,\; 1\right)$$
with joints unseen in either view set to 0.
### 4.8.  Inverse Scaling to Output Units

Finally, the internal normalized 3‑D points are converted back to **front‑view pixel units** via,
$$X^{\text{out}} = X_{\text{opt}} \cdot \frac{1}{s_f},\qquad L^{\text{out}}_b = L_b \cdot \frac{1}{s_f}.$$
Storing one consistent scale lets the visualization reproject both views using the fixed ratio,
$$\rho = \frac{s_f}{s_s}.$$
### 4.9.  Summary of Outputs

|**Output**|**Shape**|**Meaning**|
|---|---|---|
|`fused_3d_pts`|$(T, 17, 3)$|Root‑centered 3‑D joints, front‑pixel units|
|`fused_scores`|$(T, 17)$|Fused confidence per joint|
|`final_bones`|$(P,)$|Global bone lengths in front‑pixel units|

## 6.  Assumptions, Failure Modes, and Limitations

### 6.1.  Assumptions

1. **Orthographic projection.** Both cameras are modeled as orthographic. Perspective distortion is ignored. For a subject > 3 m away from the camera, error is small; for close‑ups, significant foreshortening must be corrected with a proper camera calibration.

2. **Axis‑aligned cameras.** The front camera's optical axis is assumed to lie along the world $Z$, and the side camera's along the world $X$. Oblique camera placement will rotate the recovered 3‑D cloud relative to the true world frame.

3. **Rigid skeleton.** All bone lengths are constant across the trial. Growing subjects, soft‑tissue artifacts, or compression apparel will violate this.

4. **Single person in frame.** YOLO returns the first detected pose, multiple people in the frame will mix skeletons.

5. **At least 3 frames** are required for the temporal smoothness term.

### 6.2.  Common Failure Modes

|**Symptom**|**Likely cause**|**Remedy**|
|---|---|---|
|Large, sharp $\Delta t$ but misaligned synced video|Periodic movement producing aliased correlation peak|Use a time segment with a unique transient (jump) for correlation|
|Bones drift across frames|Insufficient `bone_weight`|Increase `bone_weight`; verify reference bones are detected|
|Cyan reprojection far from grey|Heavy outlier frames or large orthographic error|Increase `robust_scale`; tighten joint validity thresholds|
|EM loop does not converge|`bone_weight` too small or data corrupted|Increase iterations; inspect `max|
|Depth (Z) collapses to zero|Only one view contributed valid joints for that region|Check YOLO detection for side view at those frames|

### 6.3.  Improvements to Consider
- **Camera calibration** with a checkerboard to replace orthographic assumption with full projective geometry.
- **Differentiable rendering** to alternate between analytic sparsity and GPU‑accelerated Jacobians.
- **Physics‑based priors** (e.g., contact constraints for feet) for closed‑chain trials like squats.
- **Per‑frame adaptive weights** derived from YOLO's temporal confidence variance.
